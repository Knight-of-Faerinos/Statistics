📝 Main Steps and Role of the Keys

  • Step 1 (Key Generation): The recipient (Bob) chooses a secret Private Key (k) and computes his Public Key (Q = kG). The public key is distributed openly, while the private key is never shared. Its role is to allow Alice to securely sync on a shared secret (S = rQ) using Bob's data, without exposing any private information.
  • Step 2 (Encryption): The sender (Alice) generates a random number (r) and creates a temporary Point R = rG. She then multiplies her random number by Bob's public key, obtaining a Shared Secret (S = rQ). Alice uses the X-coordinate of this secret to mask the message and sends Bob both the ciphertext and Point R. The role of her Ephemeral Key (r) and Point R is to act as "half of the padlock": Bob receives it and combines it with his private key to unlock the message.
  • Step 3 (Decryption): Bob receives the ciphertext and Point R. He multiplies his Private Key (k) by R to mathematically recalculate the exact same Shared Secret (S = kR). Because k * (rG) = r * (kG), Bob's private key acts as the only masterkey capable of recreating the final symmetric key. He extracts the X-coordinate and uses it to reveal the original message.

📝 Comparative Analysis: ECC vs Caesar Cipher

  • A. Encryption & Decryption Procedures: The Caesar Cipher is a symmetric monoalphabetic substitution cipher. Sender and recipient must pre-agree on a single secret key (k ∈ {1, ..., 25}). Encryption is based on a direct linear modular arithmetic operation on the alphabet letters: C = (P + k) mod 26, and decryption applies the inverse: P = (C - k) mod 26.
    The Toy ECC Scheme is an asymmetric cryptography protocol. The recipient owns a key pair (private k, public Q = kG). Encryption does not shift characters directly initially, but exploits modular geometry on elliptic curves: the sender generates a random number r, computes a public point R = rG to transmit, and derives a Shared Secret S = rQ. Decryption is only possible for the recipient through the calculation S = kR = k(rG) = r(kG).
  • B. Key Spaces: The Caesar Cipher has an extremely reduced key space, limited to only 25 possible keys (since a shift of 0 or 26 leaves the text unchanged).
    In our Toy ECC Scheme (Z_17), the private key k is restricted to the order of the cyclic group (i.e., 16 possible keys). Although on the toy curve the space is comparable to Caesar's, in a real ECC scheme (e.g., standard secp256k1) the key space is in the order of 2²⁵⁶ (approx. 1.15 × 10⁷⁷), making the key space incommensurably larger and secure.
  • C. Statistical Properties: The Caesar Cipher perfectly preserves the statistical properties of the plaintext 1 to 1. If 'E' is the most frequent letter in the plaintext, its corresponding ciphertext letter will be the most frequent. Double letters remain double (e.g., 'LL' becomes 'OO'). This makes the cipher highly vulnerable to statistical frequency cryptanalysis.
    In the ECC Scheme, although our toy implementation uses the secret point's X-coordinate to shift the text, the key generation mechanism completely hides the private key. In real ECC schemes (like ECIES or ECDH with AES), the combination of the scalar multiplication kG (which shows a strong avalanche effect) and a Key Derivation Function (KDF) completely destroys the statistical distribution of the original alphabet, producing ciphertext that is statistically indistinguishable from random noise.

📝 Effectiveness, Limitations & Alternative Approaches

  • Effectiveness: This heuristic attack is highly effective on large text corpuses because human languages follow strict mathematical distributions. By simply mapping the most frequent ciphertext character to 'E' (the most common English letter), the key is revealed instantly.
  • Limitations: It fails completely on very short messages. For example, in the word "ZOO", 'O' is the most frequent letter. A statistical algorithm analyzing just this word would falsely assume 'O' corresponds to 'E', yielding the wrong key.
  • Alternative Approaches (Optional Task): Instead of single-letter frequencies, an attacker could use an exhaustive key search (brute-force), which is trivial for computers since there are only 25 possible Caesar keys. For more complex monoalphabetic substitutions, n-gram scoring (analyzing the frequency of letter pairs or triplets, like "TH" or "AND") provides a much more robust automated attack than basic letter-frequency analysis.

📝 Attack Differences & Design Weaknesses

  • Attack Differences (Brute Force vs Statistics): The attack against the Caesar Cipher is a heuristic/statistical attack: it does not try all keys randomly, but exploits an intrinsic weakness of the cipher, namely the fact that it does not mask linguistic probabilities. If the ciphertext is long enough, the statistical attack finds the key in a single logical attempt (calculating the distance from the most frequent letter).
    The attack against our ECC scheme is instead an exhaustive brute-force attack. It does not analyze linguistic text, but solely exploits the enormous limitation of the key space (only 16 possible keys in our Toy Curve mod 17). The algorithm literally computes the operation kG for every existing number until it finds an exact match (collision) with the public key Q.
  • Why the scheme fails on a "real" curve like secp256k1: Our attack succeeds in a fraction of a millisecond because the computer has to execute the for loop a maximum of 16 times. This problem, mathematically known as the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP), is trivial on very small finite fields.
    In a properly configured ECC scheme (e.g., Bitcoin/Ethereum with the real secp256k1), the finite field p and the subgroup order are numbers close to 2²⁵⁶. This creates a key space so vast (about 10⁷⁷ combinations) that executing an exhaustive for loop to calculate every possible kG would require all the supercomputers on Earth a time longer than the estimated age of the universe. The security of real ECC derives entirely from the computational intractability of this search.
