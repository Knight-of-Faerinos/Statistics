📝 Theoretical Analysis: Riemann vs. Lebesgue

  • Methodological Difference: The Riemann integral partitions the domain (x-axis) into subintervals and sums the areas of vertical rectangles. The Lebesgue integral partitions the range (y-axis) into measurable subsets, calculating the integral by multiplying each function value by the "measure" (size) of its preimage in the domain (visualized in the histogram above).
  • Standard Convergence: For standard Riemann-integrable functions, both numerical approaches perfectly converge to the same exact value as N increases.
  • Pathological Functions (Dirichlet): The Dirichlet function highlights the superiority of Lebesgue. Riemann fails to converge because the domain cannot be neatly sliced due to infinite jumps. However, the Lebesgue approach recognizes that the measure of rational numbers is 0, and the measure of irrationals is 1. Thus, the Lebesgue integral flawlessly computes (1 × 0) + (0 × 1) = 0.

📝 Mathematical Analysis: Liouville's Theorem & Erf

  • Liouville's Theorem (1835): The Normal CDF is defined by the integral of the Gaussian function. According to Liouville's theorem, this function lacks an "elementary antiderivative". This means it is mathematically impossible to express the Normal CDF using a finite combination of elementary functions.
  • The Role of the Error Function (erf): To bypass this, mathematicians introduced a special non-elementary function called the Error Function. It provides a formal, exact analytical representation for the CDF: Φ(z) = 0.5 × [1 + erf(z / √2)].
  • The Necessity of Numerical Methods: While the erf gives us a formula, it does not solve the practical computation problem. To evaluate erf(z) for a specific number, we are forced to rely on numerical methods—such as our implementation of Simpson's Rule—to obtain highly accurate decimal results.
