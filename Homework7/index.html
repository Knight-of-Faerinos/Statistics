📝 Theoretical Framework: Statistical Independence

  • Probabilistic Definition: Two random variables X and F are statistically independent if and only if their joint probability distribution factorizes into the product of their marginal distributions: P(X = xᵢ, F = fⱼ) = P(X = xᵢ) · P(F = fⱼ) for all pairs (xᵢ, fⱼ).
  • Conditional Invariance: Equivalently, knowing the realization of X provides zero information about F: P(F = fⱼ | X = xᵢ) = P(F = fⱼ). In empirical data, this means the conditional row profiles remain invariant across all categories of X.
  • Chi-Square Test Logic: Pearson's χ² statistic aggregates the normalized squared deviations between observed counts (Oᵢⱼ) and theoretical independence counts (Eᵢⱼ). Under the null hypothesis of independence (H₀), χ² follows a chi-square distribution with df = (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom. A low p-value (< 0.05) rejects H₀, indicating significant statistical dependence.

📝 Conclusions: Association vs. Causality

  • Interpreting the Output: When the observed conditional frequencies diverge substantially from the marginal distribution, χ² grows large and the variables are statistically dependent (associated). Conversely, small fluctuations around Eᵢⱼ yield a high p-value, consistent with statistical independence.
  • Association is Symmetric; Causality is Directional: A contingency table and its χ² statistic are completely symmetric—swapping X and F produces the exact same value. Statistical dependence proves that two variables co-vary, not that X causes F.
  • Confounding & Spurious Correlations: Dependence between X and F may arise from an unobserved third variable (a confounder Z) driving both, or simply from sampling artifacts in small datasets. Establishing true causality requires controlled interventions or structural causal models rather than observational contingency tables alone.
