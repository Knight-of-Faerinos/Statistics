Law of Large Numbers (LLN) & Central Limit Theorem (CLT) Simulation

Simulation Settings

1. Total Score (Sₙ)

📝 Interpretation: Divergence and Absolute Variance

  • Behavior: As the number of systems (n) increases, the trajectories continuously spread out. Mathematically, the variance of Sₙ grows linearly with n (Var(Sₙ) = n · Var(Xᵢ)).
  • Context (n systems, m attackers): This represents the aggregate penetration score. Even though all m attackers have the exact same skill level (same probability distribution), sheer probability causes their total successes to diverge wildly over time. A larger m reveals the extreme best-case and worst-case scenarios.

2. Average Score (Sₙ / n)

📝 Interpretation: Law of Large Numbers (LLN)

  • Convergence in Probability: The Law of Large Numbers states that the sample average converges in probability to the expected value (μ). The dashed red line represents this theoretical expectation.
  • Variance Behavior: Unlike the total score, the variance here shrinks to zero as n approaches infinity.
  • Context (n systems, m attackers): Early on, an attacker might seem highly successful or incompetent due to variance. However, as the system size (n) grows, "luck" is factored out, and the aggregate penetration score stabilizes to the attacker's true underlying probability.

3. Normalized Total Score (Sₙ / √n)

📝 Interpretation: Variance Stabilization

  • Variance Behavior: By dividing by √n, we perfectly balance the growing variance of the total score. The variance remains constant regardless of how large n gets, keeping trajectories bounded within a stable horizontal "tunnel".

4. Final Distribution (Proof of CLT)

Histogram of the final Normalized Scores (Sₙ / √n) at step n for all m attackers.

📝 Interpretation: Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

  • Convergence in Distribution: According to the CLT, the sum of independent identically distributed random variables, properly normalized, converges in distribution to a Gaussian (Normal) distribution.
  • Context (n systems, m attackers): Looking at the endpoints of all m attackers at the final step n, their scores form a bell curve. This implies that while individual penetration events are discrete and random, the macro-level behavior of many attackers against many systems is highly predictable and continuously distributed.

Final Conclusions: The Predictability of Risk

Through the simulation of n systems and m attackers, we can draw three major conclusions regarding stochastic penetration processes:

  • Micro-Level Chaos vs. Macro-Level Order: At a small scale (few systems), penetration success is highly unpredictable and dominated by variance. However, as the number of targeted systems increases, the Law of Large Numbers takes over, anchoring the attacker's average success rate strictly to their true mathematical capability.
  • The Inevitability of the Bell Curve: The Central Limit Theorem reveals a profound truth: regardless of the underlying discrete model (Bernoulli or Rademacher), the aggregate normalized scores always form a continuous Gaussian distribution. This means system administrators can use standard normal probabilities to confidently calculate worst-case and best-case attack scenarios.
  • Systemic Risk is Quantifiable: While it is impossible to predict the exact outcome of a single cyber attack, the overall systemic risk of an infrastructure facing continuous threats is mathematically deterministic. Security defenses can therefore be optimized based on these stable, long-term statistical limits rather than reacting to short-term random anomalies.
