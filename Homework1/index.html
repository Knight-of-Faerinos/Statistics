    4. Coordinate Distribution and Uniformity

    Although the curve equation ($y^2 = x^3 + 7$) follows precise algebraic rules, the restriction to the finite field $\mathbb{Z}_{17}$ causes the resulting point coordinates to 'bounce' non-linearly. By analyzing the data in the table, we notice that the distribution of the x and y coordinates does not follow a linear regression (no direct correlation $y = mx + c$). Although on this 'Toy Curve' the group order is small and the points eventually form a cyclic pattern, on real curves like Ethereum's secp256k1, this behavior generates an apparent pseudo-random uniformity, which is essential to hide the private key.

    5. The Avalanche Effect

    As shown in the last column of our statistical table, a tiny change in the input (e.g., incrementing $k$ from 6 to 7) causes the flipping of a large portion of the output bits. On average, we expect a change of ~50% of the bits, ensuring a strong Hamming distance between adjacent keys. This lack of continuity prevents gradient-based attacks, making the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP) computationally intractable for large numbers.

    6. Hash Functions vs Elliptic Curve (One-Way Functions)

    A cryptographic Hash function (like SHA-256) is designed to completely destroy any original mathematical structure of the input data, returning a random fingerprint. Scalar multiplication on elliptic curves is also a One-Way function, but unlike hashes, it preserves a strong algebraic group structure. It is precisely this mathematical structure that allows the creation of verifiable digital signatures (such as Ethereum transactions), blending apparent chaos (to resist reverse-engineering) with geometric order (to allow public validation).
